Abstract
Machine unlearning, the process of efficiently removing data’s influence from trained models, has become a critical
capability for complying with data privacy regulations like the GDPRs “Right to be Forgotten.” However, the rapid
proliferation of unlearning algorithms has created a fragmented and complex landscape, making it difficult to navigate
the trade-offs between unlearning efficacy, model fidelity, and computational cost. To address this, this paper provides a
systematic and comprehensive survey of the machine unlearning field, creating a unified taxonomy and critically analyzing
foundational and state-of-the-art methods to chart a clear path for future research. Based on a systematic review of over
130 key contributions, our synthesis reveals three critical findings. First, while exact methods offer robust guarantees, their
practical application is largely confined to models designed a priori for unlearning. Second, most approximate methods
lack rigorous guarantees for deep neural networks, and their effectiveness is highly context-dependent. Third, the frontiers
of research are squarely focused on two high-impact, unsolved domains: scalable unlearning for Large Language Models
(LLMs) and privacy-preserving unlearning in Federated Learning (FL). We conclude that while machine unlearning is
a foundational pillar for trustworthy AI, significant gaps remain between theoretical promises and practical, verifiable
deployment. This survey identifies the key open challenges—including robust verification, managing the fidelity-efficacy
trade-off, and standardization—and proposes a research agenda focused on proactive, privacy-integrated, and scalable
solutions.

2 Systematic Review Protocol
To ensure a comprehensive, transparent, and reproducible
review of the machine unlearning landscape, we adopted
a systematic methodology aligned with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and MetaAnalyses) guidelines as represented in Fig. 2. This protocol
defines the scope and process of our literature search, screening, and selection.

2.1 Search Strategy and Sources
Our literature search was conducted across four major academic and pre-print databases to ensure broad coverage of
both peer-reviewed and cutting-edge research: IEEE Xplore,
ACM Digital Library, Google Scholar, and the arXiv e-Print
server. The search covered publications from the conceptual inception of machine unlearning in 2015 through early
2025. The search utilized a combination of primary and
secondary keywords, including Boolean operators, such as:
(“machine unlearning” OR “data deletion in machine learning” OR “right to be forgotten” OR “data erasure”) AND
(“federated” OR “large language models” OR “LLM” OR
“privacy” OR “security”).

3 Fundamentals of Machine Unlearning
Machine unlearning constitutes a paradigm shift from
the traditional static machine learning lifecycle, introducing the capability to dynamically manage and edit model
knowledge post-training. This section formally defines the
unlearning problem, delineates its core concepts, contrasts
it with the conventional ML pipeline, and details the critical
legal and ethical mandates that necessitate its development.

3.1 Definition and Key Concepts
At its core, machine unlearning is the process of algorithmically removing the influence of a specific subset of data from
a fully trained machine learning model, without the need to
retrain the model from scratch. Formally, let a model with
parameters θ be trained on a dataset D, which is partitioned
into a set of data to be unlearned, Df , and the remaining
data, Dr , such that D = Df ∪ Dr and Df ∩ Dr = ∅. The
“gold standard” or ideal outcome of unlearning is a new
model, hereafter referred to as the retrained model, with
parameters θretrain , which would be obtained by training
from a fresh initialization on only the remaining data, Dr .
The theoretical objective of an unlearning algorithm, U , is
to efficiently compute a new set of parameters for what we
will call the unlearned model, θu , that approximates this
ideal state as closely as possible [19]:
θu = U(θ, Df ) ≈ θretrain (1)

However, while this retraining-based definition serves as
the theoretical ideal, its inadequacy for large-scale models
is a central theme of modern unlearning research and a primary focus of this review. For contemporary architectures
like LLMs, the computational cost of generating θretrain
is prohibitive, making direct comparison impossible. More

fundamentally, for conceptual unlearning in LLMs, the
retraining ideal is often ineffective. A concept is typically
learned from countless diffuse samples across the training
corpus, not a discrete, removable subset. Even if such a
subset could be identified and removed, the model’s dense,
associative knowledge web means the concept can often be
reconstructed from remaining related information. Consequently, the practical goal of unlearning often shifts from
perfectly replicating a retrained model to the verifiable and
efficient removal of a data point’s influence, a process validated through empirical and security-based audits rather
than by direct parameter matching. The development of
such algorithms involves navigating a fundamental threeway trade-off between several key desiderata [16–18]. The
first is unlearning efficacy, which measures the degree to
which the influence of Df is successfully eliminated, often
verified by the model’s ability to resist membership inference attacks [32]. The second is model fidelity, which
ensures the preservation of the model’s predictive performance on test data. The final desideratum is computational
efficiency, as the primary motivation for unlearning is to be
significantly faster than retraining. The approaches to satisfying these criteria are broadly partitioned into two main
categories, as illustrated in the taxonomy in Fig. 1. Exact
unlearning provides a formal guarantee that θu is identical in distribution to θretrain [20], whereas approximate
unlearning relaxes this strict requirement for greater efficiency and applicability [23, 24].

3.2 Differences from Traditional Machine Learning
The concept of machine unlearning introduces several fundamental departures from the traditional machine learning
pipeline, which typically treats model training as a monolithic, one-off event [33]. Whereas traditional ML models are static artifacts, unlearning reframes the model as a
dynamic entity that can be efficiently edited throughout its
lifecycle [34], introducing concepts from continual learning such as decremental updates [35, 36]. It directly challenges the conventional assumption of data immutability by
acknowledging that data rights are fluid and require model
adaptability. This necessitates a new architectural design
philosophy, where frameworks are inherently “unlearningaware.” For instance, methods like SISA [20] and other
ensemble approaches [37] partition the training process
itself, a stark contrast to the standard paradigm. Finally, the
optimization objective itself is transformed; rather than a
singular goal of minimizing loss, unlearning introduces a
dual objective to remove the influence of Df while preserving the knowledge from Dr , a problem that can be viewed
as a form of constrained or bi-level optimization [38].

3.3 Ethical and Legal Motivations
The impetus for machine unlearning is not solely academic;
it is driven by urgent real-world requirements rooted in law
and ethics. The most significant driver is the global proliferation of data privacy regulations that codify an individual’s right to have their data erased. Translating these legal
principles into technical requirements for ML models is a
critical challenge. For instance, a legal mandate for “data
erasure” implies a technical need for “influence removal”
from a model’s parameters, while a right to “access and
correct” implies a need for model editability. This legal-totechnical translation necessitates the development of frameworks that can verifiably purge a user’s contribution from a
trained model.

It provides a mechanism to correct data errors discovered

4 Theoretical Foundations
The development of robust unlearning algorithms rests
upon solid theoretical groundings in information theory,
privacy, optimization, and statistics. This section examines
the fundamental principles that govern the formulation and
analysis of machine unlearning, establishing the mathematical and conceptual frameworks used to define, secure, and
execute the data removal process.

4.1 Information Theory in Unlearning
From an information-theoretic perspective, the ultimate
goal of machine unlearning is to minimize the mutual information between the unlearned model’s parameters, θu , and
the unlearned dataset, Df , conditioned on the remaining
data, Dr . An ideal unlearning process would ensure that
I(θu ; Df |Dr ) = 0, signifying that the final model contains
no more information about the unlearned data than a model
that was never exposed to it. This goal is formalized in some
works through a PAC-Bayesian framework, which aims to
minimize the information risk associated with the unlearned
data [49].
While this objective is clear, directly quantifying mutual
information for complex models like deep neural networks
is generally intractable. Consequently, the field has developed powerful and measurable proxies. Exact unlearning
methods, such as the SISA framework [20], achieve this
information-theoretic ideal implicitly. By ensuring the
unlearned model’s parameter distribution is identical to
that of a retrained model, they guarantee that no additional
information about Df is leaked. For approximate unlearning, the challenge lies in providing formal guarantees on
how much information might remain. The most rigorous
approaches aim for distributional indistinguishability, a
concept pioneered in [22]. This framework guarantees that
the distribution of unlearned models is statistically close to
the distribution of retrained models. This is a significantly
stronger guarantee than simple parameter proximity, as it
ensures the model’s stochastic behavior is statistically indistinguishable from a model that has truly unlearned the data.
Other theoretical approaches connect unlearning to the concept of algorithmic stability [50], arguing that if a learning algorithm is stable, the removal of a single data point
should have a bounded and predictable effect on the model,
enabling efficient unlearning. However, most approximate
methods still use parameter distance, ∥θu − θretrain ∥, or

differences in model output as more tractable proxies for
bounding this residual information flow [24].

4.2 Privacy and Security Considerations
While unlearning is motivated by privacy, the unlearning
process itself creates a new surface for privacy and security analysis [30, 51]. A central question is not just how to
unlearn, but how to verify that unlearning has been successful, particularly against a determined adversary [40, 52]. A
key threat model involves an adversary performing a Membership Inference Attack (MIA) to determine if a data point
from Df was truly unlearned. The failure of an unlearning
algorithm to defend against a powerful MIA would indicate
that discernible traces of the unlearned data remain [32]. A
seminal study in this area systematically explores the failure
modes of various unlearning algorithms, demonstrating that
common utility-based metrics can be insufficient to guarantee effective removal against such attacks [53]. Further
research has shown that even if a model is unlearned, it may
be vulnerable to targeted relearning attacks that can recover
information about the unlearned data [54, 55]. The relationship between unlearning and Differential Privacy (DP)
[56, 57] is a critical theoretical consideration. DP provides
a formal guarantee on information leakage during the initial
training phase, a concept originally explored in statistical
databases and later applied to deep learning [58]. Unlearning addresses post-hoc data removal. These concepts are
complementary and increasingly being integrated. Several
works have proposed unlearning frameworks that provide
DP-like guarantees for the unlearning process itself, ensuring that the act of unlearning a user’s data does not leak
information [59–61].
From a security standpoint, machine unlearning serves
as a critical defense mechanism. It provides a direct remedy
for data poisoning and backdoor attacks, where malicious
data is injected into the training set to compromise model
integrity [43, 45]. Foundational work on defending against
such attacks [42] laid the conceptual groundwork for using
data removal as a defense, a process now framed as unlearning [41].

4.3 Mathematical Frameworks for Unlearning
The diverse algorithms for machine unlearning are built
upon a handful of core mathematical frameworks that define
how the parameter space is traversed or reconstructed.
4.3.1 Optimization-Based Unlearning
The most direct approach frames unlearning as a continuous optimization problem. Given the parameters θ of the

original model, the goal is to find a new set of parameters
θu that approximates the minimum of the loss function over
Dr . This is often achieved by performing gradient ascent on
the loss with respect to Df [23], a method sometimes termed
“descent-to-delete” [62]. The theoretical underpinnings of
this popular fine-tuning heuristic have been rigorously analyzed, with the first formal bounds on the unlearning error
decomposing it into terms related to optimization dynamics
and approximation error [25]. Other works have explored
how factors like stochasticity in SGD influence this optimization process [63], or have developed specialized optimizers like DeltaGrad for rapid model retraining [64]. The
development of more sophisticated unlearning optimizers
can also draw from a rich history of advanced optimization
and metaheuristic research, including work on differential
evolution [65], environmental adaptation methods [66, 67],
and adaptive swarm intelligence [68].

function [69, 70], which uses a second-order Taylor expansion of the loss to approximate this change. The canonical
unlearning approach formulates the update as a single Newton-like step and was introduced for convex models in [24]:

5 Methodologies for Machine Unlearning
The practical implementation of machine unlearning is realized through a diverse array of methodologies, each offering a distinct approach to the trade-offs between unlearning
efficacy, model fidelity, and computational efficiency. This
section provides a structured overview of the principal
algorithmic families, categorized by the strength of their
removal guarantees, and concludes by detailing the critical
metrics used to evaluate their performance.

5.1 Exact Unlearning Approaches
Exact unlearning, also known as perfect unlearning, represents the most rigorous class of data removal. Methods in
this category provide a formal guarantee that the unlearned
model, θu , is identical in its parameter or output distribution
to a model, θretrain , that has been retrained from scratch
on the remaining data, Dr . This strong guarantee eliminates
the need for the complex, often inconclusive, verification
required by approximate methods, but it typically demands
specialized, “unlearnable-by-design” training frameworks.
A detailed comparison of these methodologies is presented
in Table 5.
The foundational paradigm for exact unlearning is based
on structural decomposition, most famously articulated in
the SISA (Sharded, Isolated, Sliced, and Aggregated)
framework [20]. SISA partitions the training dataset into
disjoint shards and trains an independent sub-model on
each. To unlearn a data point, only the single sub-model
corresponding to the affected shard needs to be retrained.

overhead. Subsequent research has focused on evolving this
core idea. Arcane [21] proposed a more efficient tree-based
aggregation structure for distributed systems. Deep-Split

5.2 Approximate Unlearning Techniques
In many practical scenarios, the strict guarantees and architectural constraints of exact unlearning are either too costly
or incompatible with existing model architectures. Approximate unlearning methods address this by relaxing the perfect identity constraint, instead aiming to produce a model
that is merely “close” to a retrained model. This relaxation
unlocks significant gains in efficiency and broader applicability, though it introduces the critical challenge of verifying
removal efficacy. A detailed comparison of the main paradigms is presented in Table 6.

5.2.3 The Data-Free Unlearning Scenario
Rather than a distinct mechanistic category, data-free
unlearning represents a critical unlearning scenario that
addresses the practical and privacy-enhancing constraint
that an unlearning algorithm may not access the data it
must remove (Df ). The seminal approach in this scenario
is Zero-Shot Machine Unlearning (ZS-MU) [78], which
formulates the problem as one of error correction. It estimates the parameter shift caused by Df without accessing
it, typically by training a model on a public proxy dataset to
learn the mapping from data removal to parameter changes.
This data-free approach offers substantially stronger privacy protection. Other creative data-free approaches have
also been explored, such as using an “incompetent teacher”
model that is intentionally bad at learning the unlearn class
to guide the student model on what information to discard
[84]. The primary dependency of these methods is the availability of a suitable proxy dataset or a well-crafted auxiliary
model.

5.2.4 Related Proactive and Data-Centric Methods
A distinct category of methods addresses data privacy not
by reactively removing information, but by proactively
making data difficult to learn in the first place. While not
a direct unlearning paradigm—as their primary goal is
prevention rather than removal—they are a critical part of
the broader ecosystem for trustworthy AI and are included
here for completeness. The field of unlearnable data or
“unlearnable examples” [31, 85] focuses on adding imperceptible, specially crafted noise to training data. This noise
acts as a “poison” that does not degrade model performance
on clean data but prevents the model from deeply memorizing the private information in the poisoned sample, making
its subsequent removal trivial. Another proactive paradigm
is causal unlearning [41], which frames unlearning as a
causal intervention. This is particularly useful for repairing
models after data poisoning, where the goal is to remove the
spurious causal links introduced by the malicious data.
Fig. 4 provides a conceptual mapping of various machine
unlearning methodologies within a trade-off space defined
by two critical axes: computational cost and the strength
of the unlearning guarantee. The horizontal axis quantifies the computational overhead required to perform the
unlearning operation, ranging from low to high. The vertical axis represents the rigor of the removal guarantee,
spanning from heuristic approximations at the lower end
to mathematically provable removal at the upper end. This
framework highlights a fundamental challenge in the field.
For instance, complete model retraining and SISA [20],
located in the top-right quadrant, offer provable guarantees
but are often computationally prohibitive. In contrast, the
bottom-left quadrant features more practical and efficient
heuristic methods, such as those employing gradient-based
techniques [23], zero-shot approaches [78], or influence
functions [24], which do not provide formal guarantees of
complete data removal. The ideal, yet often elusive, objective for unlearning research is to develop methods that reside
in the top-left quadrant, achieving provable unlearning with
minimal computational cost. Recent advancements, such as
PEFT-based unlearning strategies [75], represent a promising step toward this “holy grail” of efficient and verifiable
data removal.

5.4 Evaluation Metrics for Unlearning
Verifying the success of an unlearning algorithm requires
a multifaceted strategy that assesses the core trade-offs of
efficiency, fidelity, and efficacy [16, 17]. While efficiency is
straightforward to measure via Unlearning Time and memory overhead, and fidelity is assessed with standard performance metrics (e.g., accuracy) on retained data, evaluating
the efficacy of the removal is a profound challenge. As the
ground-truth retrained model is often unavailable, the field
relies on a suite of proxy metrics and security-based audits.
A common proxy is Output Comparison, where the
predictions of the unlearned model and a retrained model
(when available on a small scale) are compared on a heldout test set. For methods providing distributional guarantees, this is formalized by measuring the KL-divergence
or Total Variation distance between the models’ output

13

distributions [22]. For instance, a low KL-divergence would
imply that the unlearned model’s predictions are statistically
just as surprising (or unsurprising) as the retrained model’s,
suggesting a successful removal of the unlearned data’s
influence. Other methods evaluate the model’s confidence,
positing that an unlearned model should exhibit higher epistemic uncertainty on unlearned data points compared to
retained ones [88].
The de facto standard for security-based evaluation, however, is the Membership Inference Attack (MIA) [30]. In
a typical case study, an attacker trains a shadow model to
distinguish between the main model’s behavior (e.g., loss,
prediction confidence) on inputs it was trained on versus
inputs it has never seen. To evaluate unlearning, the attacker’s goal is to determine if a sample from the unlearn set,
Df , was part of the original training set. Before unlearning,
the MIA should achieve high accuracy, correctly identifying the sample as “member.” After a successful unlearning
operation, the model’s behavior on the unlearned sample
should be statistically indistinguishable from its behavior
on a true non-member, causing the MIAs accuracy to drop
to that of a random guess (≈ 50%). The systematic study
of these attacks is crucial, as many unlearning methods fail
against strong adversaries [53], and the effectiveness of
traditional MIAs on very large models is an open research
question [89]. Reflecting this challenge, recent work calls
for a move beyond classic MIAs towards more systematic
verification frameworks that include per-sample evaluations
and adversarial probes, which are specifically designed to
test the failure modes of unlearning in LLMs and reinforce
the consensus that robust verification is not yet a solved
problem [90].
Recognizing the need for consistency, the community is
moving towards standardized benchmarking. Efforts like

Machine Unlearning for Trustworthy AI: A Systematic Review of Techniques, Challenges, and Applications

MUSE [91] propose a multi-faceted evaluation suite for
LLM unlearning, covering aspects like efficacy, fidelity, and
even the tendency for models to “re-learn” unlearned information. Similarly, task-specific benchmarks like TOFU
[92] for LLMs and UnlearnCanvas [93] for diffusion models provide standardized datasets and tasks for reproducible
comparisons. These efforts, combined with the development
of more robust evaluation methodologies [94], are a critical
future direction for ensuring that claims of unlearning can
be rigorously and reproducibly verified.

6 Frontiers in Unlearning: Federated
Learning and Large Language Models
5.4While the foundational principles of unlearning apply
broadly, their practical application to modern, large-scale,
and distributed machine learning architectures presents a
distinct and formidable set of challenges. The monolithic,
centralized model assumption that underpins many early
unlearning algorithms does not hold in paradigms like
Federated Learning (FL) or for Massive Large Language
Models (LLMs). This section delves into these two critical
research frontiers, examining the unique problems they pose
and the specialized techniques being developed to address
them, with a comparative summary provided in Table 7.

6.1 Federated Unlearning
Federated Learning (FL) is a distributed learning paradigm
where numerous clients collaboratively train a shared global
model under the coordination of a central server, without
ever sharing their local, private data. This privacy-preserving setup introduces a unique unlearning requirement: if a
client decides to withdraw its participation, its entire data
contribution must be verifiably erased from the collaboratively trained global model. The naive solution—retraining the entire federation from scratch without the departed
client—is computationally infeasible and negates the efficiency of the FL process, as detailed in recent surveys on
the topic [27]. This has given rise to the subfield of Federated Unlearning. The core challenge is to remove a client’s
influence from the central model efficiently while respecting
the privacy of the remaining clients [98].
6.1.1 Approximate Federated Unlearning
Early and common approaches are approximate in nature,
focusing on efficiency over perfect removal. A prominent
technique is to use knowledge distillation, where the aggregated model from the remaining (non-departed) clients acts
as a “teacher” model. A new, clean “student” global model
is then trained to mimic the output distribution of this
teacher, thereby learning the collective knowledge without
the influence of the unlearned client [86]. While practical,

6.1.2 Exact Federated Unlearning
While efficient, approximate methods do not provide formal guarantees. A distinct line of research focuses on exact,
provable removal. One approach, exemplified by Federaser [100], requires the server to store all historical model
updates from every client. To unlearn a client, the server
removes all of that client’s historical updates and re-aggregates the model from the remaining history, often with a
partial retraining of the final layers. While this is exact, it
imposes a significant storage burden on the server. A more
sophisticated cryptographic approach, Exact-Fun [77],
uses primitives like additive secret sharing. Here, clients
provide cryptographic shares of their updates, and the server
aggregates the shares without seeing the raw updates. To
unlearn, the server simply excludes the departed client’s
shares and reconstructs the model perfectly from the rest,
offering exactness with strong privacy but incurring higher
communication and computational overhead.
6.1.3 Privacy-Preserving Unlearning
A further consideration is the privacy of the unlearning
process itself. The act of unlearning client A could leak
information about client A’s data. To mitigate this, frameworks like FedRecovery [60] integrate differential privacy
directly into the unlearning mechanism. By injecting calibrated noise during the corrective update steps, they provide
formal guarantees that the unlearning operation itself does
not reveal private information, at the cost of a slight, controlled degradation of model utility.

6.2 Unlearning for Large Language Models (LLMs)
Large Language Models represent the ultimate challenge
and, arguably, the most urgent application for machine
unlearning. Trained on web-scale datasets that are often
poorly documented and may contain copyrighted or private
information [101], these models are not only astronomically expensive to retrain but are also known to memorize
and regurgitate sensitive content [102, 103]. The need to

13

surgically remove such content post-training is therefore a
critical component of responsible AI development, a topic
explored in a growing body of dedicated surveys [28, 29,
104, 105].
Unlearning in LLMs is exceptionally difficult due to a
confluence of factors. First is the sheer scale of the models, making Hessian-based calculations intractable. Second
is distributed knowledge; information in LLMs is not
localized but is represented in a complex, distributed manner across millions of parameters, making precise removal
akin to neurosurgery [106, 107]. Finally, there is a significant risk of catastrophic unlearning, where an imprecise
operation damages unrelated knowledge [95, 108].
Current research is dominated by three main approximate
paradigms:
6.2.1 Gradient-Based Steering
This is the most common approach, which treats unlearning
as a fine-tuning problem. The most direct method is gradient ascent, where the model performs several optimization steps to maximize the loss on the data to be forgotten,
effectively pushing the model parameters away from the
undesirable knowledge [95]. A more refined strategy frames
unlearning as a preference alignment task. Using techniques
like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), the model is
trained on preference pairs where responses containing the
unlearned information are explicitly marked as “rejected,”
teaching the model to avoid generating such content. This
approach, termed Negative Preference Optimization,
offers more targeted control than simple gradient ascent
but can suffer from instability if not carefully implemented
[108, 109].
6.2.2 Knowledge Alignment
This paradigm uses an auxiliary “teacher” model that
already lacks the knowledge to be unlearned. The unlearning process then involves fine-tuning the original model (the
“student”) to align its output distribution with that of the
teacher. The alignment can be enforced at the output layer
(via KL-divergence) or on internal representations. The
core mechanism of the KGA framework, for instance, is to
minimize this “knowledge gap” between the two models,
effectively distilling the desired behavior from the teacher
[96]. The primary limitation is the need to procure or create a competent teacher model, which can itself be a costly
process.

6.2.3 Surgical Model Editing
Rather than retraining, this approach aims for precise, surgical modification of the model’s weights. Research has shown
that factual knowledge in transformers is often localized
within specific feed-forward layers [106]. Surgical methods leverage this insight to identify the “privacy neurons”
or parameter blocks responsible for storing the unwanted
information and then directly ablate or modify their weights
[110, 111]. While computationally efficient, these methods
face the challenge that knowledge is rarely perfectly localized, and such edits may not fully erase all distributed traces
of the information.
Despite this active research, the field is still in its infancy.
A general consensus, highlighted by multiple critiques, is
that “we are not there yet” [97]. Many existing methods
are brittle, failing against sophisticated adversarial checks
[55, 112]. Indeed, recent multi-faceted benchmarks suggest
that most current methods still leak private content, degrade
model utility, or fail under sequential unlearning requests
[91]. Developing scalable and truly verifiable unlearning for
foundation models remains one of the most significant open
problems in machine learning [113, 114].

7 Challenges in Machine Unlearning
Despite significant progress, the practical, widespread
deployment of machine unlearning is hindered by several
formidable challenges. These challenges span the entire
spectrum from algorithmic efficiency and scalability to the
fundamental tension between unlearning and model utility,
and the unresolved problem of verifiable erasure. This section delineates the most critical open problems facing the
field, grounding them in the mathematical and algorithmic
principles discussed previously.

7.1 Computational Efficiency
A core promise of machine unlearning is to be significantly more efficient than retraining a model from scratch.
Maintaining this efficiency advantage, especially as model
complexity grows, remains a major challenge. For exact
unlearning methods that rely on the sharding principle introduced by SISA [20], the primary challenge is the overhead
of the retraining step. While faster than a full retrain, this
cost can still be substantial, in addition to the initial setup
and potential inference latency costs inherent to the ensemble-based architecture.
For approximate methods, particularly those based
on closed-form perturbation, the primary computational

bottleneck is the Hessian matrix. The canonical Newtonstep update for a unlearn set Df is given by:

7.2 Scalability to Large Models
The scalability of unlearning algorithms to modern, largescale architectures like LLMs is arguably the most pressing challenge today [28, 29]. The sheer size of these models
amplifies the efficiency challenges to an extreme degree.
The complex, non-convex loss landscapes of deep neural networks violate the strong convexity assumption,

of what is provably possible in non-convex settings [116].
Furthermore, knowledge in LLMs is known to be highly
distributed and non-localized across parameters, making the surgical removal of specific information a delicate
operation [106]. An imprecise unlearning update, such as
those performed via gradient ascent, can cause catastrophic
unlearning of unrelated but functionally similar knowledge, degrading model capabilities in unpredictable ways
[97, 108]. While specialized methods are emerging [95, 96,
117], developing scalable unlearning techniques that are
both effective and safe for foundation models is a grand
challenge.

knowledge learned from Dr . This trade-off is particularly
acute in approximate unlearning. The theoretical bounds
on gradient-based methods, for instance, show an explicit
trade-off where a larger unlearning step (i.e., learning rate)
reduces one error term while increasing another, highlighting the sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning [25].
This challenge is exacerbated by the “coreset effect,”
where a small number of highly influential data points
can have a disproportionately large impact on the decision
boundary and overall model performance [118]. Recent
work on identifying “worst-case unlearn sets” demonstrates
that unlearning certain data combinations is significantly
harder and more damaging to model utility than unlearning others [119]. The problem of identifying what makes a
unlearn request “hard” before attempting to execute it is a
complex and largely unsolved issue [120]. Developing principled algorithms that can navigate this trade-off space—for
example, by maximizing erasure subject to a constraint on
fidelity loss is a key open research direction, echoing challenges found in multi-objective reinforcement learning and
adaptation [121].

7.4 Verification of Unlearning
Perhaps the most profound challenge in machine unlearning
is the problem of verification: how can one be certain that
the unlearning process was successful? The absence of the
ground-truth retrained model, θretrain , makes direct comparison of the parameter distance between the unlearned
model and the ideal retrained model, ∥θu − θretrain ∥
impossible in practice.
While a suite of evaluation metrics exists, none are foolproof. The current gold standard, Membership Inference
Attacks (MIAs), provides a security-based audit but has its
own limitations. The effectiveness of an MIA depends heavily on the power of the attack model and the assumptions it
makes. Indeed, an unlearning algorithm may successfully
defend against a weak MIA while remaining vulnerable to a
stronger, more adaptive one, leading to a false sense of security [53]. The very applicability of traditional MIAs to the

13

statistical patterns of LLs is also an active area of research
[89].
This creates a “cat-and-mouse” dynamic, where the
development of unlearning algorithms must constantly
anticipate more sophisticated verification attacks, such
as targeted relearning attacks [54] or knowledge recovery
probes [55]. Establishing a universally accepted, computationally feasible, and theoretically sound verification protocol—what some have termed an “auditable algorithmic
definition for machine unlearning” [40]—is a critical unmet
need. Without one, the promises of unlearning algorithms
remain difficult to substantiate, hindering their adoption in
high-stakes, trust-critical applications.

8 Current State of Research
The field of machine unlearning has transitioned from a
theoretical curiosity into a dynamic and essential area of
machine learning research. A significant body of work now
exists, but this rapid growth has also brought the field’s limitations and open challenges into sharp relief. This section
synthesizes the current state of research by highlighting key
milestones, summarizing the primary limitations of existing
methods, and identifying the most critical research gaps that
remain to be addressed.

8.1 Key Contributions and Milestones
The trajectory of machine unlearning research, as illustrated
in Fig. 5, can be traced through several landmark contributions that have defined its core paradigms. The field was first
conceptualized as a necessary component for data management in ML systems [15], which was followed by the first
rigorous, practical framework for exact unlearning, SISA,
demonstrating that perfect, provable removal was possible
through algorithmic design [20]. A major breakthrough
for approximate methods was the introduction of certified removal [24], which established that unlearning need
not be purely heuristic by providing the first formal (ϵ, δ)
-guarantees on parameter-space proximity for convex models. The focus of these guarantees was soon elevated from

Machine Unlearning for Trustworthy AI: A Systematic Review of Techniques, Challenges, and Applications

parameter similarity to the more robust notion of distributional indistinguishability [22], which ensures the entire
stochastic behavior of the unlearned model matches that
of a retrained one. Recognizing the practical and privacyrelated drawbacks of requiring access to the unlearned data,
the zero-shot paradigm was introduced, creating a new
and highly practical subfield focused on data-free removal
[78]. Most recently, research has firmly expanded to modern architectures, with a growing body of work tackling the
unique unlearning challenges in Federated Learning [27, 60,
87] and, most critically, Large Language Models [28, 29],
which represent the current frontier of research.

8.2 Limitations of Existing Approaches
Despite these milestones, every major class of unlearning
methodology carries significant limitations. The perfect
guarantees of architectural methods like SISA come at the
cost of high rigidity and significant computational overhead,
as detailed in Section 5.1. This often makes them inapplicable as a post-hoc solution for conventionally trained models [20]. In contrast, while approximate unlearning is more
flexible, its methods often lack robust guarantees for deep
learning. As detailed in Section 7, the reliance of influencebased techniques on a strong convexity assumption remains
a major barrier to their provable application in deep learning [24, 71]. Meanwhile, the guarantees for more common
gradient-based methods are still emerging and demonstrate
a high sensitivity to hyperparameter choices [25]. Finally,
the elegant data-free approach of zero-shot unlearning introduces a strong dependency on a public proxy dataset whose
distribution must match the private training data, a property
that is difficult to verify in practice [78]. Across all categories, a universal limitation remains the lack of a definitive,
computationally feasible method for verifying unlearning
success, as reliance on audits like Membership Inference
Attacks may provide a false sense of security against stronger adversaries [53, 55].

8.3 Open Problems and Research Gaps
The limitations of current methods point directly to the
most pressing open problems and research gaps. The foremost challenge is developing efficient, effective, and safe
unlearning algorithms for foundation models. This requires
moving beyond convexity assumptions to understand how
to perform precise surgery on highly distributed knowledge
representations without causing catastrophic side effects
[97]. This is part of a broader theory-practice gap; there
is a critical need to bridge the formal guarantees derived
in simplified settings with the practical realities of applying unlearning to complex, non-convex models. Another

key gap is in the principled quantification and management of trade-offs between unlearning efficacy, fidelity,
and cost [18]. Frameworks are needed to navigate this space
and understand the underlying factors that make certain data
harder to unlearn [119, 120].
Furthermore, the development of a standardized, scalable, and trustworthy protocol for robust verification is a
grand challenge. This likely requires moving beyond a pure
MIA-based approach to a combination of formal methods,
statistical tests, and security audits that can provide a more
holistic certificate of removal [40, 52]. Finally, a promising
research direction lies in shifting from reactive to proactive
unlearning. Instead of only developing methods to remove
data post-hoc, the paradigm of creating unlearnable data
[31] seeks to design data from the outset to be inherently
easy to remove or difficult to memorize. Addressing these
gaps will be essential for transforming machine unlearning
from a collection of specialized algorithms into a mature
and indispensable component of the standard machine
learning toolkit.

9 Future Directions
As machine unlearning matures, its trajectory will be
defined by its ability to overcome its current challenges
and integrate with broader scientific and societal ecosystems. The future of the field lies not only in refining existing algorithms but also in exploring new paradigms, forging
interdisciplinary connections, and establishing the standards
necessary for widespread, trustworthy adoption.

9.1 Emerging Trends
Several emerging trends are poised to shape the next generation of unlearning research. A significant development is
the shift from reactive removal to proactive, unlearnableby-design systems. This includes the creation of “unlearnable data,” where imperceptible perturbations are added to
training inputs to prevent deep memorization or to create
vulnerabilities that allow for trivial, low-cost removal [31,
85]. This philosophy flips the problem on its head: instead
of asking how to unlearn, we ask how to learn in a way
that makes unlearning easy. Another key trend is the move
towards compositional unlearning. As models become
more complex, the need extends beyond removing simple
data points to erasing abstract concepts, features, or entangled facts [122, 123]. For LLMs, this could mean unlearning a specific factual error by editing knowledge neurons
[124] or using task arithmetic [125] to subtract a particular
behavior, a task that requires a much deeper understanding
of how knowledge is represented. Finally, the future lies

13

R. Kumar et al.

in integrating unlearning as a first-class citizen within the
Machine Learning Operations (MLOps) lifecycle. This
points towards automated unlearning (AutoU) frameworks
that can trigger, execute, and verify removal requests as part
of a continuous deployment pipeline, selecting the optimal
strategy based on model architecture and performance constraints, a task analogous to complex resource optimization
challenges in cloud workflow scheduling [126] and hybrid
energy systems [127].

9.2 Potential Interdisciplinary Connections
The growth of machine unlearning will be significantly
enriched by drawing formalisms from adjacent scientific
fields. There is immense potential for developing neuroinspired unlearning algorithms by taking cues from cognitive neuroscience. Concepts such as synaptic pruning and
catastrophic interference in the human brain could provide
biologically plausible mechanisms for selectively removing
information from artificial neural networks without destabilizing the entire system [23]. The verification challenge
aligns closely with the goals of cryptography and formal
methods. Future work could use cryptographic tools, such
as zero-knowledge proofs [128], to allow a model owner to
formally prove that an unlearning operation was performed
correctly without revealing the model’s parameters.
A critical dialogue with legal and ethical scholarship
is also essential to translate ambiguous principles like the
RTBF into precise, machine-testable specifications [129].
Questions regarding what level of residual MIA accuracy
constitutes a legal failure to unlearn, or the legal standing
of models trained on copyrighted data [130], cannot be
answered by computer scientists alone. This collaboration
is necessary to ensure technical solutions are aligned with
societal expectations and to navigate the complex landscape
of “algorithmic disgorgement” as an enforcement tool à la
[39].

9.3 Policy and Standardization Needs
For machine unlearning to transition from a research topic
to a trusted industrial practice, a concerted effort in policy
and standardization is required. The field currently lacks
a unified benchmark for comparing algorithms, creating a
need for standardized benchmarks and leaderboards.
The community would benefit from developing comprehensive datasets and tasks, such as UnlearnCanvas for diffusion models [93] or TOFU for LLMs [92], into a broader
“Unlearning-GLUE” style suite. This would accelerate
progress by enabling fair and reproducible comparisons of
efficiency, fidelity, and efficacy. Furthermore, there is a need
for clear certification and auditing frameworks. To build

13

trust, standards are required for what constitutes a “certified”
unlearning process [52], specifying the types of evidence an
organization must provide to demonstrate compliance. This
moves beyond trusting the algorithm and towards a system
of verifiable claims, a necessity highlighted by research
into the insufficiency of black-box audits [131]. Finally, the
development and maintenance of high-quality, open-source
toolkits that implement key unlearning algorithms are crucial for lowering the barrier to entry, promoting best practices, and serving as reference implementations for these
emerging standards.

10 Conclusion
The rapid ascent of machine unlearning from a niche theoretical problem to a critical field in trustworthy AI reflects a
fundamental shift in our relationship with machine learning
models. As these models become more powerful and more
deeply integrated into society, the need to control, edit,
and correct them is no longer a luxury but a necessity. This
paper has provided a comprehensive survey of the machine
unlearning landscape, charting its course from foundational
principles to the frontiers of modern research.
This review has systematically charted the landscape
of machine unlearning, establishing its core motivations
in data privacy, model maintenance, and regulatory compliance. We have delineated the fundamental dichotomy
between exact unlearning, which offers perfect, provable
erasure at the cost of architectural rigidity, and approximate unlearning, which provides a flexible and efficient
alternative but often relies on heuristic methods or assumptions that are violated in deep learning.
Our survey has traversed the key methodological milestones, from the structural guarantees of SISA [20] and the
formal certificates of influence-based methods [24], to the
distributional guarantees of hybrid approaches [22] and the
enhanced privacy of the zero-shot paradigm [78]. We have
examined the unique and formidable challenges posed by
applying these techniques to complex, large-scale domains
like federated learning and Large Language Models, which
represent the current and future battleground for the field.
Our analysis has shown that despite significant progress,
major challenges remain in scalability, the management of
performance trade-offs, and, most critically, the verifiable
auditing of unlearning efficacy.
For practitioners and engineers in the industry, this
review serves as a guide to a rapidly maturing toolkit. The
key takeaway is that designing systems to be “unlearnableby-design” is vastly more effective than treating unlearning
as a post-hoc patch. As organizations deploy new ML systems, particularly those handling sensitive data, they should

Machine Unlearning for Trustworthy AI: A Systematic Review of Techniques, Challenges, and Applications

proactively consider architectures and training strategies
that facilitate efficient data removal. While a universal, onesize-fits-all solution does not yet exist, the methodologies
discussed herein offer a range of options with clear tradeoffs, enabling more informed decisions about model lifecycle management.
For researchers, this review highlights a field rich with
open problems and opportunities for high-impact work.
The path forward involves moving beyond incremental
improvements to tackle grand challenges. These include
developing scalable and provable unlearning methods for
foundation models, closing the gap between theory and
non-convex practice, creating robust verification protocols
that can withstand adversarial scrutiny, and exploring novel,
interdisciplinary connections with fields like cryptography
and neuroscience. The ultimate goal is to elevate unlearning
from a collection of disparate algorithms to a unified, rigorous, and indispensable science.
Machine unlearning is more than just a technical solution
to the “Right to be Forgotten”; it is a foundational component for building adaptable, secure, and trustworthy artificial intelligence. It represents a move away from static,
immutable models towards dynamic systems that can be
corrected, controlled, and aligned with human values over
time. The challenges ahead are significant, but the progress
made thus far is a testament to the ingenuity of the research
community. As we continue to push the boundaries of what
machine learning can achieve, the ability to selectively and
efficiently unlearn will be just as important as the ability
to learn. The continued pursuit of this goal is essential for
ensuring that the next generation of AI is not only powerful
but also responsible.
