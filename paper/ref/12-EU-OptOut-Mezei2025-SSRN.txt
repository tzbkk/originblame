and the AI Act – For the Benefit of Whom?
(v3.0)

(Lithuania); Adjunct professor (dosentti), University of Turku (Finland)

Abstract:
The rapid development of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI), the trendiest type of artificial
intelligence (AI) these days, prompts legislation around the Globe to (re)consider the need for and
the details of regulatory solutions to manage the copyright consequences of this phenomenon. The
European Union (EU) – like in other fields of law – is in the forefront of regulation.
On the one hand, the EU’s Copyright in the Digital Single Market Directive introduced a specific
text- and data-mining (TDM) exception for the benefit of research organisations and cultural
heritage institutions (Article 3), as well as any user (Article 4). The latter exception might be
directly relevant for GenAI model developers and providers, including its provisions on the
reservation of rights. Under this, rightsholders might ‘opt out’ their works and other protected
subject matter from TDM. The CDSM Directive did, however, not conclusively clarify how, when,
in what exact form and by whom such reservation might be exercised. In any case, these rules, so
far, imagined a collaboration via the flagging of reserved contents by rightsholders and,
consequently, the respect of such opt-outs by GenAI providers.
On the other hand, the AI Act, published in the Official Journal in July 2024, will affect the
functioning of such rights reservation mechanisms by introducing an obligation to put in place a
policy document on how GenAI developers comply with rights reservation rules [Article 53(1)(c)]
and to draw and make publicly available a sufficiently detailed summary on the data used for
training purposes [Article 53(1)(d)]. Furthermore, the newly established AI Office will step in with
its administrative role and duties to oversee the compliance with the AI Act, especially in the form
of a Code of Practice. Consequently, the formerly clear private law setting has now been
supplemented with some administrative, public law features.
Currently, however, the efficacy of the EU’s multi-layered legislation is far from being certain.
The present chapter aims to review how these layers of norms will ultimately be put into practice
and co-exist in the European Union.

2.2. What is the reservation of rights in essence?
There are various conceptual elements of the commercial TDM exception that might need to be
discussed in great depth. The first of these can easily be sidestepped at the moment (but will be
revisited shortly in subchapter 3.1). The EU acquis is drafted flexibly to allow for the transposition
of Article 4 in either the form of an exception or a limitation. Yet, the EU Member States have
typically introduced remuneration-free exceptions rather than paid but permitted limitations, thus
rendering moot any debate about this categorization dilemma.23

of rights; and no effect on scientific TDM per Article 3 CDSM Directive. The majority of these

a futile exercise, it looks necessary to flag that the term ‘opt-out’ is flawed (even if it is a mere
metaphor).
On the one hand, the rights reservation does not put rightsholders in a position that is ‘external’ to
their rights; they do not only ‘rule out’ some user behaviour. Indeed, foreclosing TDM practically
puts rightsholders back to an ‘internal’ position, that is, where they might require the authorization
of and payment for commercial TDM activities. In other words, Article 4(3) might rather be called
an ‘opt-back’; or as Hanjo Hamann coined, an ‘exception-exception’.25
Viewed from a contractual perspective, a rights reservation is neither an offer, nor an acceptance;
hence it represents no solid ground for an authorization yet. Paul Keller coined rights reservation
– viewed from model trainers’ perspective – an ‘expression’ or ‘statement’ of intent that the trainers
shall respect.26 Indeed, it looks like a mere ‘non-acceptance’ of the non-authorized (and non-paid)
use of protected subject matter. As such, it is only an expression of the will of the rightsholders to
negotiate the terms of authorization with the users;27 opening the doors for individual or – possibly
– collective rights management.
This semantic clarification is an important starting point for the upcoming discussions, as it clearly
highlights the position that rightsholders and users might have. The formers must be in the position
to express their will on the banning of non-authorized TDM towards users; who, in turn, shall not
take TDM ‘granted’ and/or look at rights reservation as a simple ‘exiting’ of contents from GenAI
developers’ activities. To the contrary, they shall get ready to authorize the use of the training data.
3. The AI Act and rights reservation
Following a long debate – and several revisions in its substance –, the AI Act has been published
during the summer of 2024. Originally, it was aimed not to include any words on the copyright
aspects of AI. Policymakers were, however, prompted to reflect on the accelerated development
of GenAI, and hence the final version included a few, yet relevant provisions with a sight on
copyright law. Indeed, these provisions have direct relevance for the purposes of our discussion.28
Before discussing the relevant provisions of the AI Act and the freshly founded AI Office’s draft
Code of Practice, we shall cast our eyes on a crucial point of the regulation. Namely, its copyrightrelevant provisions oblige only GPAI model29 providers,30 who differ from GPAI system31
providers. As Quintais puts, “the model is the engine, whereas the system is the car one drives on
the road”.32 In other terms, the models represent the underlying knowledge that systems are built
on to ultimately serve customers. The evident example here is the distinction between GPT and
ChatGPT; where the former is the model that the latter system relies on. Since current GenAI
applications (systems) are not necessarily developed by the model providers, the AI Act’s ultimate

3.1. Article 53(1)(c) AI Act on a policy to comply with EU copyright law
Article 53(1)(c) rules that GPAI model providers shall “put in place a policy to comply with Union
law on copyright and related rights, and in particular to identify and comply with, including
through state-of-the-art technologies, a reservation of rights expressed pursuant to Article 4(3) of
Directive (EU) 2019/790”. This provision is based on two premises: first, the requirement to ‘put
in place’ a policy document; and, second, to identify and comply with the reservation of rights,
which, in essence, requires GPAI model providers to adhere to the CDSM Directive.
As regards the first prong, ‘putting in place’ shall mean the complex and recurring task of GPAI
model providers to draw up, publish and keep such policy document up-to-date, as well as to
effectuate their commitments. Publishing the policy shall be understood in a broad sense: the
document shall be accessible to the general public, rather than solely to the AI Office. This is
evident from the language and structure of the AI Act itself. In Article 53(1)(a), the AI Act
introduces a limited publication obligation (‘upon request, to the AI Office and the national
competent authorities’) regarding the technical documentation of GPAI models; whereas Article
53(1)(c) does not include any such limitation.
As regards the second prong, GPAI model providers’ policy, in line with effet utile,34 shall be
meaningful. This does not mean that the policy document itself shall create the framework for any
rights reservation; the policy document is definitely not the right means for that. Yet, the document
shall not hinder rightsholders in effectively reserving their rights in line with the state of the art
technologies, including, but not limited to GPAI model providers’ internal solutions.35 This is even
more true in light of the scope of the AI Act and the CDSM Directive. The latter introduced the
rights reservation requirement with websites hosting data used for training purposes in mind, rather
than model providers that scraped data from those websites.36
Article 53(4) AI Act leaves it to the GPAI model providers to choose from three alternatives as
regards the compliance with the previous obligation. They might follow the AI Office’s Code of
Practice (to be accepted by early May 2025); or – once and in case it is published – a harmonized
standard; or they might individual demonstrate their alternative compliance mechanism to the
European Commission.37 It is to be seen, which approach will turn to become the default one.
At the moment, we are only aware of the AI Office’s third draft Code of Practice that provides
guidance both on the scope and modalities of the policy requirement per Article 53(1)(c); and the
modalities and methods of the rights reservation mechanism that will be considered compliant with
Article 53(4) AI Act. The Internet Engineering Task Force chartered a working group in February
2025 to carry out a standardization process;38 but this process has no visible output yet. Also, Open

The first draft of this document stressed that the policy shall cover the entire lifecycle of the model;
signatories of the Code of Practice shall diligently request information from any third party they
intend to conclude a contract with about the use of data for the development of GPAI models
whether and how these third parties complied with the rights reservation requirement (upstream

The second draft of the Code of Practice developed the internal logic of the prescribed
requirements, and encapsulated various novel elements at the same time. As such, e.g. it listed
already 11 measures with an overall 16 key performance indicators. The document – with more
streamlined language – stuck to the fundamental obligations related to drawing up and
implementing an internal copyright policy. It also included numerous straightforward proposal,
like requesting GPAI model providers to make reasonable efforts to assess whether third-party
datasets complied with copyright law (measure 2.3); to commit ensuring lawful access to the
training data (measure 2.4); not to crawl pirate websites (measure 2.5); to commit to reservations
expressed through the Robots Exclusion Protocol (robots.txt) (measure 2.6); to identify forms of
appropriate expressions of rights reservations other than robots.txt and make best efforts to comply
with the reservations expressed through those technologies (measure 2.7); or to prevent overfitting
(measure 2.9) are all significant steps toward a robust, clear and standardized regulatory

Article 53(1)(d) prescribes that GPAI model providers “draw up and make publicly available a

4. Developing European case law on text- and data-mining
Although this chapter reviews the EU approach to TDM and rights reservation, we cannot but
admit that neither the EU nor its Member States can present much practical insight into these topics
yet. The vast majority of practical developments are either only loosely connected to our topic,
stem from other jurisdictions or are due to non-EU corporations. Nevertheless, there are already
(to the best of my knowledge) three court decisions related to rights reservations in Europe. The
first reactions of commentators, however, seem to suggest that the case law did not contribute to
the effective operationalization of the EU provisions.68

4.3. Gamekapocs – web-scraping as TDM?
The latest TDM decision in the European Union was made in Hungary. This ruling deserves close
attention, as the Municipal Court of Appeals had to decide – among others – whether the scraping
of plaintiff’s website by Google for purposes of indexing the relevant contents fits into the general
purpose TDM exception.
In its 3 December 2024 judgment, the Municipal Court of Appeals reversed the trial court’s refusal
of such defence, and concluded that “[i]t was not disputed that the defendant had lawful access to
the plaintiff’s press publications and did not circumvent the technical measures taken by the
plaintiff to access them. Moreover, the robots exclusion protocol assigned to the plaintiff’s website
allowed all search robots, including the defendant’s search robot, to crawl and index, and the
plaintiff did not object to the indexing in the form required by law. The robots exclusion protocol
is not of significance for consent, but primarily for the fact that it is – in accordance with the
provisions of the Copyright Act – a machine-readable form by which the right holder could object
to the text- and data-mining by the search engine. Since the defendant does not save the download
of the page required for crawling, as it does not keep a copy of the page, and the data and
information contained in the index are not copies, the statutory condition in Article 35/A(1)(c) [of
the Copyright Act] is also satisfied”.97
In short, the Hungarian ruling declared web-scraping / search engine indexing ‘a form’ of TDM.
Although any such judgment aligns with the aims of the third draft of Code of Practice, it raises
considerable concerns as regards its reasonableness.
First, although the CDSM Directive defines TDM in a broad, technical sense, 98 the telos of the
directive was not to exempt every automated data analysis from liability under Article 4. Indeed,
the recitals of the directive make it clear, on the one hand, that the “processing of large amounts of
information with a view to gaining new knowledge and discovering new trends possible”99 might
be carried out for research purposes (per Article 3 CDSM Directive) or for “government services,
complex business decisions and the development of new applications or technologies” (per Article
4 CDSM Directive).100 None of these options seem to align with the decades old practices of webscraping by search engines. On the other hand, the preamble of the directive also expressly notes
that “there can also be instances of text and data mining that do not involve acts of reproduction
or where the reproductions made fall under the mandatory exception for temporary acts of
reproduction provided for in Article 5(1) of Directive 2001/29/EC”,101 in which case Article 4
CDSM Directive cannot be applied. Even more, Article 4(2) also notes that reproduced or extracted
information might only be retained as long as they are necessary for the purposes of text- and data"machine-readable" means, The IPKat, February 16, 2025 (https://ipkitten.blogspot.com/2025/02/dutch-court-holdsthat-tdm-opt-out-must.html).

Second, the inclusion of web-scraping into the concept of general purpose TDM might run afoul

subjects them to a single legal provision.102 This does not generate any clearer legal situation, but,
to the contrary, makes the interpretation and application of the CDSM Directive even more risky.
Namely, general purpose TDM – as presented in recital 18 – was in line with the three-step test,
especially since it guarantees rightsholders the opt-out right under Article 4(3). To the contrary:
the application of this rule to the single most important technology that Internet browsing is based
upon surpasses the limits of ‘certain, special cases’. Any such interpretation of Article 4 would
simply make the exception a general rule. Furthermore, the third step (‘unreasonable prejudice to
the legitimate interests of the rightsholders’) speaks against the court’s ruling for at least two
reasons. Namely, since rightsholders, who host their data on their websites, are typically interested
in the indexing of their contents for browsing purposes, hence they let bots scrape their sites.
However, they do not necessarily want to let commercial companies use their contents for purposes
other than indexing. Declaring indexing and automated content analysis a single concept would
make rightsholders lose their ability to make decisions related to technologies with distinct scope
and aims.103 Also, closely connected to that, with any such merger, rightsholders would lose their
express statutory right – guaranteed by Article 4(3) CDSM Directive – to opt-out from TDM;
whereas indexing has been for long seen as the backbone of search engines, an activity that was
exempted from liability per the safe harbours of e-commerce law, hence hardly contested by
anyone.104
5. (Not a real) Conclusion
This chapter cannot end with a conclusive statement. It would be indeed hard to answer the
question raised by the chapter’s title itself: who is the real beneficiary of the new rights reservation
regime of the EU?
To the contrary, the rights reservation dilemma is far from being fixed. As shown above, there are
some slightly clearer answers to a limited number of sub-questions, yet, the original hypothesis,
namely, that Article 4(3) CDSM Directive is a ‘complex equation’ still stands. We cannot but agree
with Paul Keller’s point of view, who argued that there will most probably be no one-size-fits-all
and flexible enough solution in this matter.105
